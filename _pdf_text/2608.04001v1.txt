                                        1     Introduction
                                        Large language models (LLMs) often become substantially more capable on difficult reasoning tasks when
                                        they are allowed to externalize intermediate reasoning steps and consume additional computation at inference
                                        time. Early work on chain-of-thought prompting and self-consistency showed that arithmetic, symbolic, and
                                        commonsense reasoning can improve markedly when models generate multi-step solutions and aggregate
                                        multiple samples (Wei et al., 2022; Wang et al., 2022). More recent systems treat deliberate reasoning as an
                                        explicit design target rather than a prompting artifact: DeepSeek-R1 uses reinforcement learning to elicit
                                        long-form reasoning (Guo et al., 2025); Phi-4-reasoning combines curated supervised traces with outcome-
                                        based reinforcement learning (Abdin et al., 2025); Qwen3 exposes explicit “thinking” modes and reasoning-
                                        budget controls (Team, 2025); and the gpt-oss release provides open-weight reasoning models trained with
                                            1 For the API list, see Appendix F; for the release data, see Appendix G.




                                                                                                       1
large-scale distillation and reinforcement learning (OpenAI, 2025). As a result, reasoning performance is now
shaped not only by pretraining and post-training, but also by how computation is allocated at test time.
Despite this progress, the phrase test-time scaling is used to describe several materially different inference
algorithms. Some methods allocate extra compute along a single evolving trajectory, for instance, by forcing
longer deliberation or adaptively intervening on one response (Muennighoff et al., 2025). Others sample
multiple completed candidates and reduce them using voting, reranking, verifier-based selection, or minimum-
Bayes-risk decoding (Wang et al., 2022; Cobbe et al., 2021; Freitag et al., 2022). Still others search over
unfinished partial states, as in Tree-of-Thoughts, RAP, and value-guided decoding (Yao et al., 2023; Hao
et al., 2023; Yu et al., 2024). These regimes differ in their statistical structure, induced proposal distributions
over candidates, compute accounting, and failure modes. Treating them as interchangeable under a single
scalar “budget” obscures the actual procedure being evaluated and makes cross-paper comparisons difficult
to interpret (Welleck et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2025).
Evaluation is correspondingly more subtle than benchmarking a base model in isolation. In practice, mea-
sured performance depends on the full inference protocol: the prompt template, decoder, search controller,
reducer, verifier or judge, stopping rule, and numerical settings. Best-of-N and verifier-guided reranking
can improve accuracy, but imperfect proxy scores may themselves be overoptimized as the candidate set
grows (Gao et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025; Khalaf et al., 2025). LLM judges provide flexible evaluation of
open-ended outputs, yet prior work documents biases such as position and verbosity effects, and recent work
shows that judge quality is highly task- and procedure-dependent in test-time scaling settings (Zheng et al.,
2023; Zhou et al., 2025). Even when prompts and methods are fixed, benchmark estimates can vary due to
stochastic decoding and implementation details, motivating interval estimates and explicit reproducibility
controls (Miller, 2024; Blackwell et al., 2024; Ye et al., 2024; Liu et al., 2025b). A rigorous study of rea-
soning under test-time scaling thus requires a precise algorithmic vocabulary, evaluation protocols matched
to deployed inference algorithms, and model comparisons that are explicit about both model-side training
choices and inference-time controls.
This need for explicit system-level evaluation is amplified by the pace and heterogeneity of the open-weight
reasoning ecosystem. Recent releases span reinforcement-learning-first reasoning models (Guo et al., 2025),
data-centric and verification-focused SFT pipelines (Ye et al., 2025; Guha et al., 2025), open reproductions
built around released trace corpora and training code (Hugging Face, 2025a; Open-R1 Team, 2025b), and
hybrid systems that combine reasoning and non-reasoning behavior either within a single checkpoint or in
parameter space (Team, 2025; Yang et al., 2025). This diversity is scientifically valuable, but it also makes
comparisons prone to confounding: two checkpoints may differ simultaneously in their supervision signals,
reasoning styles, compute budgets, and aggregation protocols. The field, therefore, benefits from studies
that separate these axes and evaluate reasoning as a property of the full inference system rather than of
model weights alone. Figure 1 summarizes this shift as a selective chronology: inference-time algorithms,
model-side training, and interface controls have co-evolved toward systems whose performance depends on
the checkpoint, interface, controller or reducer, evaluator, stopping rule, and budget.
This paper addresses that gap. We formalize test-time scaling as a family of budgeted inference algorithms
over the implicit prefix tree of an autoregressive model. Our treatment of sampled inference separates the
evidence computed for each trace from decisions on a completed pool; causal stopping is modeled sepa-
rately because it changes generation cost. Shared-bank comparisons can then isolate aggregation, whereas
end-to-end evaluation measures the complete inference system. Candidate-bank quality is reported through
structured diagnostics, including a unified family of repeated-sampling metrics for the trade-off between dis-
covery and stability. We instantiate these ideas on a broad set of contemporary open-weight reasoning models
and assemble over 2 million reasoning traces to support trace analysis, verifier training, and reproducible
study across science, reasoning, and competition mathematics.

2   Formalizing Test-Time Scaling

Test-time scaling is most naturally viewed as a family of budgeted inference algorithms built atop a fixed
autoregressive model. Recent work studies repeated sampling with terminal reduction, adaptive single-


                                                                                               3
trajectory deliberation, and search over partial reasoning states as distinct ways of spending additional
inference-time computation with a fixed base model (Welleck et al., 2024; Snell et al., 2025; Muennighoff
et al., 2025; Wu et al., 2024). The common object behind these methods is the implicit prefix tree induced
by generation: extra compute may be spent along a single trajectory, across multiple completed leaves, or
over internal prefixes before completion. We formalize that view and use it to distinguish three structural
regimes.

2.1   Problem setup and three-regime taxonomy

Let x ∈ X be an input prompt and let pθ (· | x) be an autoregressive language model. The finite prefixes
reachable from x define an implicit rooted tree T (x); we write z ⪯ y when prefix z lies on the path to
completed generation y, and L(x) for the set of terminal leaves (Welleck et al., 2024). A local generation
policy π (for example, greedy decoding, temperature sampling, or nucleus sampling) induces a proposal
distribution qπ (y | x) over completed leaves, which may differ from the raw model distribution because
decoding can truncate, renormalize, or otherwise transform token probabilities.
A completed generation y may contain both intermediate reasoning and a task answer. We write Parse(y) =
(r, a), where r is the reasoning trace and a is the extracted answer. When only part of the output can
be checked programmatically, we further write Ψ(y) = (d, s), where d is the deterministically verifiable
component and s is the remainder. Depending on the task, d may be the final answer string, an executable
program, an action sequence, or any other artifact that admits task-specific checking.
Our object of interest is a family of budgeted inference algorithms {AB }B∈B . Algorithm AB may adaptively
interleave primitive operations ot such as token generation, prefix  expansion, verifier calls, judge calls, or
terminal reduction, subject to a cumulative cost constraint t c(ot ) ≤ B. It returns either a completed
                                                                P
leaf ŷB (x) ∈ L(x) or an extracted answer âB (x). Let Ux (y) denote the task utility of a completed leaf. In
reasoning benchmarks Ux (y) is often exact correctness, but the same abstraction also covers graded utilities
such as execution score or preference reward.
In practice, Ux is usually observed only through evaluation signals. A programmatic verifier VP (x, d) ∈ V
may return a Boolean decision, a scalar score, a diagnostic object, or a canonicalized answer representation. A
learned evaluator Jϕ (x, ω) scores either a completed leaf ω = y or a partial state ω = z. Unlike programmatic
checks, learned evaluators are proxies whose usefulness depends on their supervision and on the protocol
used at test time (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025).
For a task distribution P, the associated test-time scaling curve is

                                       G(B) = Ex∼P, ξ Ux (ŷB (x; ξ)) ,

where ξ denotes the algorithm’s internal randomness, or the corresponding answer-level utility when the
method outputs âB (x). The term scaling refers to how performance varies with budget; it does not imply
that performance must be monotone for every method.
We use the following operational taxonomy, illustrated in Figure 2.

Single-trajectory sequential scaling. At every step, there is at most one unfinished active prefix. Ad-
ditional computation changes how that prefix is extended, revised, or terminated, but never branches into a
competing frontier.

Leaf-level scaling. Additional compute produces a bank of completed candidates. Any interaction be-
tween candidate trajectories is deferred until after the candidates have completed, via a terminal reducer.

Prefix-level scaling. Additional compute is allocated based on scores for unfinished prefixes. Expansion,
pruning, rollout allocation, or termination decisions depend on partial states before completion.
These regimes are not mutually exclusive at the system level: many practical methods are hybrids, most
commonly prefix search followed by a leaf-level reducer.

2.2   Single-trajectory sequential scaling

A single-trajectory method maintains one active state zt and remaining budget bt . At step t, a controller
chooses a meta-action
                                         νt ∼ πseq (· | x, zt , bt ),
then extends only the active path,

                                  zt+1 ∼ Extend(zt ; νt , pθ ),               bt+1 = bt − c(νt ).

Typical actions include continuing generation, suppressing eos, appending a control string or critique, chang-
ing decoding hyperparameters, or stopping. Budget-forcing methods such as s1 are direct instances of this
template, and more general inference-time meta-generation procedures also fit it when they keep a single
evolving response rather than a branching frontier (Muennighoff et al., 2025; Welleck et al., 2024).
The structural advantage of this regime is low orchestration overhead: all extra compute is devoted to a
single evolving candidate. Its structural limitation is that, because no competing unfinished prefix exists,
recovery from an early misconception requires explicit self-revision along the same path rather than searching
for alternatives.

2.3.2   Failure modes and budget allocation

The main advantage of leaf-level scaling is that increasing compute increases candidate coverage without
risking early pruning. Its failure modes come from the reducer. An incomplete verifier can admit false posi-
tives as N grows, while misspecified learned scores can be overoptimized by hard argmax selection, producing
the non-monotone Best-of-N behavior documented in recent work on reward-model overoptimization and


                                                            6
inference-time reward hacking (Gao et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025; Khalaf et al., 2025). Judge-based reduc-
ers also inherit protocol sensitivities such as position bias and verbosity bias, so judge design and prompting
are part of the method under evaluation (Zheng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025). Finally, leaf count and
evaluator count are distinct budgets: MBR-style reducers may require O(N 2 ) utility calls, motivating ap-
proximate implementations such as confidence-based pruning and faster MBR approximations (Cheng &
Vlachos, 2023; Jinnai & Ariu, 2024).
A fixed sample count is therefore only one leaf-level design choice. One may instead choose N = N (x) by

2.4     Prefix-level scaling: search over partial states

Prefix-level scaling allocates compute before trajectories are complete. To cover both token-level search and
step-level methods such as Tree-of-Thoughts, RAP, and AlphaZero-like decoding, let z denote a search state
representing either a literal token prefix or a macro-prefix composed of one or more reasoning steps, and
write Succ(z) for its allowed expansions (Yao et al., 2023; Hao et al., 2023; Wan et al., 2024b).

2.4.1    Frontier, terminal bank, and budget

A prefix-level method maintains an active frontier Ft , a bank of completed leaves Yt , and remaining budget
bt . A generic iteration is

                      zt ∈ Select(Ft ) → Ct ⊆ Succ(zt ) → score Ct → (Ft+1 , Yt+1 , bt+1 ).

The budget may count generated tokens, node expansions, rollout calls, verifier or judge calls, or wall-clock
cost. Unlike in leaf-level scaling, future compute allocation depends explicitly on evaluations of unfinished
states.

prefix score is the continuation value

2.4.5    Scaling behavior and comparison to leaf-level methods

Prefix-level search can be markedly more computationally efficient when prefix scores concentrate budget
on high-value branches. Its additional difficulty is that pruning decisions driven by prefix scores change
the support of reachable completions. If every ancestor of a correct leaf is pruned, no later computation
can recover that leaf. Consequently, the relevant statistical property is often not global calibration but the
within-instance ordering induced over competing frontier states.
Under a fixed budget B, prefix search also induces its own effective proposal distribution over completed
leaves,

Finally, prefix search creates large branching structures with shared prefixes. Efficient tree-structured infer-
ence kernels exploit this sharing through key-value cache reuse, which can materially change the practical
compute frontier; DEFT is a representative example (Yao et al., 2025). Many real systems are therefore
hybrid: prefix search first constructs a search-induced bank of completed leaves, and a leaf-level reducer then
selects the final output. The repeated-sampling metrics used later to summarize such candidate banks are
introduced in Section 3.4.

3     Evaluating Test-Time Scaling

Under test-time scaling, reported performance depends on the full inference protocol defined over the prefix
tree in Section 2.1. The protocol determines both the candidate distribution and the final output rule.
Its specification includes budget allocation, decoding or search, inference-time evidence, aggregation and
stopping rules, and any verifier or judge calls. Evaluation should therefore state the benchmark structure,
the induced proposal distribution, the aggregation and stopping protocol, the reported estimand, and the
uncertainty procedure.

3.1     Benchmark design as evaluation structure

Benchmark design determines which capability is measured and whether additional inference-time compute
can meaningfully change outcomes. Under test-time scaling, benchmark suites should therefore be organized
by evaluation structure, not only by task domain or historical popularity.
A primary distinction is between verifiable and open-ended tasks. In verifiable settings such as mathematics
and code generation, correctness can be checked exactly or by execution, so repeated sampling and ter-
minal reduction target a comparatively sharp correctness criterion (Chen et al., 2021; Li et al., 2022). In
open-ended settings, evaluation relies on rubrics, pairwise preferences, or judge models; consequently, the
evaluation protocol itself—including prompts, comparison order, side randomization, and aggregation—is
part of the method being evaluated (Liang et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023; Chiang et al., 2024). In so-
cially consequential tasks, the evaluation target extends beyond task utility to fairness, with data-level,
training-time, and post-processing mitigation strategies evaluated using both predictive-performance and


                                                        9
group-fairness metrics (Kiashemshaki et al., 2025). These regimes interact differently with additional com-
pute: verifier-based selection, agreement-based aggregation, and judge-based re-ranking need not improve
at the same rate as budget increases (Cobbe et al., 2021; Lightman et al., 2023; Zheng et al., 2023).
Because open-weight reasoning checkpoints differ materially in verified supervision, preference objectives,
and built-in reasoning-budget controls, Section D.2 summarizes these system-level levers separately.
The benchmark structure should also match the task’s interaction model. Single-turn exact-answer tasks
probe discovery of a correct terminal leaf, whereas interactive agent benchmarks evaluate full trajectories,
including recovery from intermediate errors (Yao et al., 2024; Welleck et al., 2024). For interactive chatbot
tasks involving sensitive disclosures, ecological validity also requires modeling users’ contextual privacy deci-
sions and the protective actions exposed by the interface (Nezhad et al., 2026). The same inference algorithm
can therefore appear strong under “any correct sample” reporting yet weak under trajectory-level reliability
metrics (Section 3.4).
Freshness and difficulty calibration matter especially under test-time scaling. Benchmark-label integrity
is also essential, as vision-language-model-based sanitation of noisy and semantically inconsistent labels in
manufacturing datasets can materially affect downstream classification accuracy (Mahjourian & Nguyen,
2026). Contamination-limited benchmarks and time-segmented datasets help distinguish genuine search or
verification gains from memorization (White et al., 2025; Jain et al., 2025; Oren et al., 2024). Benchmarks
should also avoid saturation: if tasks are too easy, additional compute has little effect; if they are uniformly
difficult, scaling curves flatten because correct solutions are rarely reachable. Since many reasoning bench-
marks are small, dataset size directly limits the statistical resolution of scaling comparisons (Snell et al.,
2025; Miller, 2024; Ye et al., 2024) (see Section 3.5).

to low-scoring candidates (Taubenfeld et al., 2025; Kuang et al., 2026). With hard maximization, exposure
to proxy-score errors increases as the bank grows (Gao et al., 2023; Huang et al., 2025).

uses only previously designated traces, with tuning data kept separate from the test set. Cost accounting
includes warm-up samples, discarded samples, and runs in which the stopping condition never fires. Any
other replay uses information or compute unavailable to the deployed controller.

Comparison and compute accounting. A shared-bank comparison measures aggregation conditional
on one completed bank. Each offline protocol receives the same serialized candidates and only the inference-
time metadata it is permitted to use. Holding Eη , parsing, canonicalization, candidate eligibility, and
score transformations fixed isolates Gδ ; otherwise, those differences belong to the compared post-generation
protocols. If both evidence and decision components vary, the comparison covers the full post-generation
stage (Eη , Gδ ) under a fixed proposal and realized bank. Neither variant can estimate savings from adaptive
stopping or changes caused by a different generation policy. Generalization beyond the realized bank still
requires uncertainty estimates over prompts and candidate draws. An end-to-end comparison instead allows
each complete protocol to generate its own stream, acquire its own evidence, execute its stopping rule, and
be evaluated by MB .

post-generation aggregation stage, and holding evidence fixed narrows attribution to the decision rule. The
discovery–stability profile in Section 3.4 remains complementary because it describes candidate availability
under the proposal, not the success probability of a particular aggregation protocol.

3.5   Reproducibility

Under test-time scaling the object that must reproduce is a randomized inference protocol, not a model
checkpoint. Reproducibility norms in machine learning and natural language processing ask whether inde-
pendent researchers can obtain compatible results from the stated code, data, and procedure, and whether
reported scores carry the development traces, budgets, and variability needed to interpret them (Pineau
et al., 2021; Dodge et al., 2019; Bouthillier et al., 2021). A leaf-level run draws, or causally stops while
constructing, a candidate bank from the proposal qπ (· | x) induced by the prompt, decoder, and budget
(Section 3.2). It computes inference-time evidence and applies a fixed-bank decision (Section 3.3) before
scoring the submitted output or candidate bank (Section 3.4). The bank is therefore one realization of a
Monte Carlo experiment. Replaying that realization and reproducing the quantity it estimates are different
goals. Exact replay reconstructs the same candidate texts, inference-time signals, stopping and aggregation
decisions, and reported scores from the same items, weights, tokenizer, chat template, decoding configuration,
random-number streams, software stack, and hardware; it underwrites auditing, debugging, and regression
testing. Distributional reproducibility instead asks that an independent rerun of the declared protocol return
statistically compatible estimates of the same estimand, such as MB , the profile Sk , or a judge-defined win
rate. Such a claim presupposes a model of the variation an honest rerun would exhibit. Fixing a seed makes
one transcript auditable but characterizes neither the variability over prompts, candidate draws, and judge
calls nor that of numerical execution; treating a single seeded run as evidence of replicability, and varying
only the seed as a complete uncertainty analysis, are recurring errors (Bethard, 2022).
This variation has both statistical and procedural sources (Miller, 2024; Blackwell et al., 2024), and each
enters at a distinct stage of the prefix-tree pipeline. The proposal is fixed by every prefix-local decoding


                                                       16
choice, including temperature, the ordering of truncation gates, deterministic token actions, stopping and
length rules, and any adaptive budget controller. These settings constitute qπ rather than merely implement
it (Section 3.2). The candidate bank need not be i.i.d.: samples that share prefix-search state, adaptive stop-
ping decisions, verifier feedback, tool state, or a scheduler-dependent random-number stream are dependent,
which voids the independent-attempt reading that the repeated-sampling diagnostics of Section 3.4 rely on.
Generation does not fix the interpretation or evaluation map. Answer extractors, canonicalizers, unit-test
harnesses, judge prompts, and pairwise orderings can treat the same completion differently. The position,
verbosity, and protocol sensitivities of LLM judges then affect either the aggregation rule or the benchmark
metric, depending on where the judge is used (Zheng et al., 2023; Zhou et al., 2025). Numerical execution
is likewise not inert: when logits are close, differences in precision (Hariri et al., 2026b), kernels, or batching
can flip the emitted token and propagate it along a long reasoning trace, so even greedy decoding need not
be deterministic (Hochlehnert et al., 2025; Yuan et al., 2025), and the resulting run-to-run variability in
reasoning outcomes should be measured directly rather than masked by a high average score (Liu et al.,
2025a).
The checkable artifact is the bank together with its provenance: rendered prompts, raw completions, parsed
answers, token- or step-level metadata, verifier and judge transcripts, transformed scores, stopping and
aggregation decisions, and the per-candidate random stream. Releasing these records, as in transparent
evaluation frameworks and recent reproducibility audits, lets others separate raw candidate quality from the
behavior of the aggregation and stopping protocol (Liang et al., 2023; Hochlehnert et al., 2025). The protocol
also determines the estimand. The sample budget N , temperature, evidence map, aggregation and stopping
controls, extraction rules, and seed schedule must therefore be declared before outcomes are inspected.
Changing them after seeing results turns evaluation into selection on the test set, and any validation-set
tuning belongs in the report rather than in the headline number. Sample identities should be fixed before
execution through a deterministic map from run, prompt, sample index, and stream name to a counter-based
random state. This keeps asynchronous batching from changing which variate is attached to each candidate.

that matches the reported quantity. For a binary outcome zq ∈ {0, 1} with dataset success rate p̂ =

Numerical and serving settings. Numerical execution must therefore be specified as precisely as de-

settings, since low-bit quantization in particular can degrade reasoning on the hardest items (Liu et al.,
2025b). An FP32 or higher-precision audit on a subset of items then separates algorithmic from numerical
variation (Yuan et al., 2025). Model-state integrity is an additional serving-side concern: bit flips can corrupt
language-model parameters and outputs, and lightweight tensor-auditing methods have been proposed to
detect and recover such faults at inference time (Tahmasivand et al., 2025).

4     Empirical Study and Trace Corpus

Our empirical study uses fixed response banks to measure how quickly additional sampling reveals a correct
candidate and how often practical reducers select one. The release retains the responses, outcomes, and
recorded signals, so new reducers can be evaluated without regenerating responses. Its four blocks span
different tasks, model rosters, sampling protocols, and levels of signal detail (Table 1). We therefore report
each block separately.

4.1     Broad evaluation across knowledge and symbolic reasoning

This block covers all 14 MMLU-Pro domains (Wang et al., 2024) and the 23 BIG-Bench Hard tasks (Suzgun
et al., 2023). We evaluate 27 open-weight reasoning models with one zero-shot chain-of-thought response
per question, yielding 500,661 responses. MMLU-Pro exact match ranges from 3.13% to 70.47% across the
roster, while BBH flexible-extraction exact match ranges from 25.97% to 82.20%. We report the two suites
separately because their tasks, answer spaces, and parsers differ. The complete roster, prompts, and file
census appear in Section G.3.

4.2     Candidate discovery outpaces answer selection

4.4              Reference-assisted and reference-free verifier signals

The signal-rich blocks record one reference-assisted score and one reference-free score. CompassVerifier-3B
(OpenCompass) receives the question, reference answer, and candidate response and predicts A/B/C for
correct, incorrect, or invalid responses (Liu et al., 2025c). We retain both its direct label distribution and a
null-baseline-adjusted contextual distribution. The reference-dependent score is an outcome diagnostic and
cannot be used for inference-time selection when gold answers are unavailable.
Our reference-free score is a pointwise adaptation of the pairwise LLM-as-a-Verifier method (Kwok et al.,
2026). Qwen3.6 sees only one problem and one candidate response. It receives no reference answer or ground
truth, Compass result, or other candidate. It separately critiques problem understanding, reasoning validity,

and whether the conclusion is supported, then assigns each criterion an ordinal A–T score. We map A through
T to 20 through 1, take the expected ordinal score under the returned, visible-mass-renormalized scoring-
token probabilities, normalize it to [0, 1], and average the three criteria. This signal can rank candidates
without a gold answer and it is an ordinal evaluator score. The exact prompts, decoding passes, score
equations, context handling, and audit counts are given in Section G.6.
For SuperGPQA, each bank likewise contains 80 responses per question across broad graduate-level knowl-
edge (M-A-P Team et al., 2025). We construct a field-balanced set by selecting 50 questions from each of
72 EvalScope fields, for 3,600 questions total (ModelScope Team, 2024). The four banks consist of Qwen3.6
and three gpt-oss conditions at low, medium, and high effort. The release includes the full trace, width-20
token alternatives, rule-based multiple-choice outcome, and both verifier records.


5   Conclusion

Test-time scaling is a family of budgeted inference algorithms. Viewing these algorithms over the implicit
prefix tree separates single-trajectory deliberation, leaf-level sampling and reduction, and prefix-level search,
while exposing the proposal, evidence map, decision rule, and causal stopping controller as distinct compo-
nents of the inference process. The scientifically meaningful object of comparison is therefore the complete
inference system.
This perspective naturally determines how reasoning systems should be evaluated. End-to-end utility and
candidate-bank diagnostics answer different questions; shared-bank and end-to-end comparisons support dif-
ferent claims; compute accounting must include generation, evaluation, control, and decision; and uncertainty
must propagate through the deployed inference protocol. Our framework also unifies repeated-sampling
evaluation through the discovery–stability profile and distinguishes exact replay from distributional repro-
ducibility. The accompanying corpus comprises 1,948,821 full reasoning traces spanning broad knowledge,
symbolic reasoning, and competition mathematics, including token-level candidate log probabilities and two
verifier families in the signal-rich blocks.
Our empirical study demonstrates the practical consequences of this system-level view. Rankings change
with the rubric, sampling budget, benchmark, and commitment standard; runtime signals that predict
correctness within trajectories need not reliably rank models; and strict extraction can conflate abstention
with error. Our results argue that progress in test-time scaling should be measured using reproducible
inference systems whose inference protocols, compute budgets, and uncertainties are explicitly reported,
rather than by checkpoints evaluated under incompletely specified procedures.

C     Prefix-level search: controller and evaluator variants

Prefix search allocates computation using scores on unfinished states, with three consequences for evaluation:
local evaluation and control factorize into separate design choices, search shifts the proposal distribution over
leaves, and prefix methods consume compute on axes that are invisible if one reports only the number of
completed candidates.


                                                             37
C.1   Evaluator–controller factorization

A prefix-level search procedure can be written as a pair (S, πctrl ), where S maps a partial state to a score or
summary statistic and πctrl uses those summaries to choose meta-actions,

                                           at ∼ πctrl (· | x, Ft , Yt , bt ).

The action at may specify which node to expand, how many successors to generate, how many rollouts to
allocate, which states to prune, or when to terminate. Beam search, best-first search, Tree-of-Thoughts-style
search, RAP, and MCTS-style procedures all instantiate this evaluator–controller pattern with different
scorers and control rules (Kocsis & Szepesvári, 2006; Browne et al., 2012; Yao et al., 2023; Hao et al.,
2023). This factorization is useful in ablations: improvements may come from better local evaluation, better
compute allocation, or both.

C.2   Search-induced proposal shift

Any controller together with its scorer and stopping rule induces an effective leaf distribution

which generally differs from the decoder-induced proposal qπ (y | x); when the search returns a bank rather
than a single leaf, the corresponding object is the per-leaf inclusion probability of the returned bank (Sec-
tion 2.4). Prefix search is therefore not only a compute allocator but also a proposal shaper. This matters for
evaluation because banks returned by search are correlated and biased toward branches that the controller
explored. Threshold metrics such as Pass@k and the profile coordinates Sbk,t bank

puted on such banks, but they should be interpreted as descriptive summaries of the search-induced bank
unless the experimental protocol explicitly averages over repeated executions of the full search algorithm.
When S is a learned reward or value model, prefix search inherits the familiar problem of proxy misspecifica-
tion and adds a branch-elimination failure mode: a promising branch may be pruned before any high-utility
leaf from that branch is ever generated. This failure mode has no direct analog in pure leaf-level reranking,
where the candidate already exists before selection (Gao et al., 2023; Hung et al., 2025).

C.3   Compute accounting

Prefix search incurs compute costs for partial-state scoring, rollouts, and tree maintenance, in addition to
final generations. Reporting only the number of completed leaves can therefore be misleading: two methods
may return the same number of leaves while using very different amounts of model computation and evaluator
computation. At minimum, empirical comparisons should separate generated-token cost from evaluator cost,
and note whether specialized systems support shared-prefix execution. This distinction matters because
tree-structured kernels can exploit prefix sharing through key–value cache reuse, substantially changing the
wall-clock frontier for search-based methods (Yao et al., 2025).


D     Reasoning LLMs and Evaluated Models

Recent open-weight LLMs have produced a distinct class of reasoning models trained or configured to emit
intermediate reasoning steps for multi-hop problems in mathematics, science, and code. In practice, these
gains rarely come from additional pretraining alone; they arise primarily from model-side adaptation and
inference interfaces: adapting a pretrained backbone to structured problem solving using explicit reasoning
traces, verifiable rewards, or preference signals, and configuring how reasoning is elicited or budgeted at infer-
ence time. Figure 1 gives the main-text chronology connecting these developments to inference-time scaling;
this appendix provides the detailed taxonomy and representative open-weight model descriptions used in
our evaluation, emphasizing the design choices that most directly affect reasoning accuracy, robustness, and
inference cost.


                                                          38
D.1   Common Post-Training Pipeline Archetypes

Recent open-weight reasoning pipelines largely fall into three recurring post-training archetypes. Parameter-
efficient adaptation has also been extended to decentralized settings, where clients exchange low-rank updates
without centralizing their private data (Ghiasvand et al., 2025).
(1) SFT→RL pipelines. A common recipe first applies supervised fine-tuning (SFT) on curated chain-
of-thought (CoT) (Wei et al., 2022) demonstrations to elicit explicit multi-step reasoning, then uses rein-
forcement learning (RL) to optimize behavior under a reward signal. The reward may reflect verifiable
correctness (e.g., exact-match math or unit-test code), human or model preferences (e.g., helpfulness or
format adherence), or efficiency objectives such as conciseness to mitigate overthinking. This paradigm un-
derlies systems such as Sky-T1/Flash (NovaSky Team, 2025b;a;c), Light-R1/TinyR1 (Wen et al., 2025;
Sun et al., 2025), and Phi-4 reasoning variants (Abdin et al., 2025), where post-training explicitly trades off
correctness, reasoning length, and style.
(2) Distillation-based pipelines. Distillation treats reasoning acquisition primarily as a data-generation
and filtering problem: a strong teacher (e.g., DeepSeek-R1 (Guo et al., 2025) or QwQ (Qwen Team,
2025a)) produces reasoning traces, which are then transferred to a smaller student by standard cross-
entropy SFT. Compared with RL, distillation is typically easier to reproduce and much cheaper to train,
while still transferring the teacher’s reasoning style and decomposition heuristics. Open workflows such
as Bespoke-Stratos (Bespoke Labs, 2025b), OpenThoughts/OpenThinker (OpenThoughts Team,
2025c), and Open-R1 (Hugging Face, 2025a; Open-R1 Team, 2025e) exemplify this line: they scale ei-
ther the amount of verified reasoning data (from ∼10K to > 1M examples) or the rigor of filtering (e.g.,
exact-match checks and unit tests), often achieving large gains without specialized RL.
(3) Parameter-space fusion and merging. A third line avoids gradient-based updates and instead
fuses multiple pretrained or post-trained experts directly in parameter space. Modern merging rules, such
as Select–Calculate–Erase (SCE), compute structured weighted combinations of aligned weights so that a
single merged model inherits complementary behaviors from its parents, such as long-CoT mathematical
reasoning and shorter, more direct responses. This approach underlies the FuseO1 family and related
FuseAI releases (Wan et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2025), and also appears in branch–merge schemes such
as TinyR1 (Sun et al., 2025). Fusion is attractive when training budgets are tight because it can be
performed quickly and without requiring new data, although its effectiveness depends heavily on checkpoint
compatibility.

D.2   Cross-Cutting Mechanisms and Inference Controls

While Section D.1 describes recurring pipeline archetypes, we compare reasoning systems along four cross-
cutting mechanism classes used in Figure 1 and Table 2: verified SFT and trace distillation, RL and preference
optimization, inference-time control and aggregation, and parameter-space composition.
CoT distillation and verified SFT. At the low-compute end, CoT distillation transfers solution decom-
positions from a strong teacher to a student by standard SFT. The dominant factor is not raw data volume
but the informativeness and correctness of supervision, including the reliability of the annotators who pro-
vide labels (Ghiasvand et al., 2026); reasoning pipelines also use rejection sampling and verification, such as
exact-match math checks or unit tests for code, before training. This yields compact but high-yield corpora
that can reliably improve reasoning over strong instruction-tuned bases (NovaSky Team, 2025b; Bespoke
Labs, 2025b; OpenThoughts Team, 2025c).
RL and preference optimization. When additional generalization or behavioral control is needed, RL-
style post-training can optimize policies directly for correctness or preference-aligned behavior. In open-
weight work, this often appears as outcome-based RL in verifiable domains such as math and code, or as
preference optimization to reduce overthinking and stabilize response length. Direct Preference Optimization
(DPO) (Rafailov et al., 2023) is widely used as a practical alternative to Reinforcement Learning from Human
Feedback (RLHF) (Ouyang et al., 2022) because it avoids an explicit reward model and value function while
still optimizing a preference objective. In reasoning models, such objectives often prefer shorter correct
chains over longer ones, or penalize redundant verification, as in Sky-T1-Flash (NovaSky Team, 2025a).


                                                      39
Inference-time scaling and aggregation. Reasoning accuracy is also sensitive to the allocation of
inference-time compute. Test-time scaling methods sample multiple candidate solutions and aggregate them,
for instance, by majority vote in self-consistency, to improve reliability without changing weights (Wang et al.,
2022). Other controls allocate a “thinking budget” by encouraging longer deliberation or constraining it to
reduce latency, thereby trading off cost and robustness. Because these procedures modify the effective deci-
sion rule beyond the base conditional distribution, they should be treated as part of the evaluated system
and reported explicitly.
Model merging and fusion. Finally, parameter-space fusion provides a non-gradient mechanism for com-
bining complementary reasoning behaviors, such as long-CoT competence and concise answering. FuseAI-
style systems (FuseAI, 2025a;b) use heterogeneous model fusion to produce merged checkpoints that often
behave like a single hybrid expert, without multi-stage training runs (Wan et al., 2024a; Yang et al., 2025).
In this setting, fusion is especially relevant because it can substantially alter reasoning length and style while
keeping the backbone family and parameter count fixed.

D.3    Comparison of Representative Open-Weight Reasoning Models

We instantiate this taxonomy on families represented in, or directly contextualizing, the evaluated roster.
Table 2 summarizes the representative open-weight reasoning models examined in this work, organized by
mechanism class, training-data scale, base model, and the technical choices that most directly affect both
accuracy and cost.
The models evaluated span the principal open paradigms used in modern reasoning LLMs, including verified
SFT, SFT+RL/preference optimization, inference-time scaling controls, and parameter-space fusion. This
coverage matters for evaluation because different paradigms induce different failure modes and cost pro-
files: distillation and verified SFT tend to be stable and reproducible; RL and preference optimization can
improve correctness or conciseness but are sensitive to reward construction and verification; inference-time
scaling improves reliability but increases test-time compute; and fusion can alter reasoning style substantially
without additional data. Consequently, any meaningful benchmarking must report both the model check-

performance even when weights are fixed.

D.3.1    DeepSeek R1 and distilled students: RL-first reasoning and high-yield distillation

DeepSeek-R1 demonstrates that multi-step reasoning can be induced by large-scale RL on verifiable tasks.
Guo et al. introduce DeepSeek-R1-Zero, trained with RL directly from a base model (no SFT warm-
start), which exhibits emergent self-verification and reflection but produces less readable outputs and may
mix languages. DeepSeek-R1 addresses these issues with a multi-stage recipe that introduces cold-start
data prior to RL and combines SFT and RL stages (including rejection sampling within the overall pipeline)
to preserve reasoning while improving instruction-following and output quality (Guo et al., 2025).
The DeepSeek-R1-Distill family transfers R1 behavior into standard dense backbones rather than
running RL directly on small policies. The released students include Qwen2.5-based checkpoints at
1.5B/7B/14B/32B, and a Llama-3.1-based 8B checkpoint (Guo et al., 2025). The Hugging Face model
cards report that the Qwen2.5 distilled students are fine-tuned on ∼800K DeepSeek-R1-curated samples,
positioning distillation as a compute-stable alternative to small-model RL (DeepSeek-AI, 2025a). The ac-
companying project documentation further argues that distilling reasoning patterns from a strong teacher is
more effective than rediscovering them via RL at small scales, supporting distillation as a standard small-
model alternative in open-weight settings (DeepSeek-AI, 2025b).

D.3.2    Qwen reasoning models: RL-scaled thinking, long-context, and sparse MoE

Qwen’s reasoning line treats “thinking” as an explicit post-training target and emphasizes architectural
efficiency (long context and sparse MoE) for models intended to emit intermediate reasoning by default.
QwQ-32B is a dense 32B reasoning model trained using RL; the Qwen team reports competitive performance
against contemporary reasoning systems such as DeepSeek-R1 and o1-mini (Qwen Team, 2025e;a). This
provides an RL-first reference point at moderate scale, separate from large distillation pipelines.
Qwen3 extends this direction by separating thinking and non-thinking behaviors and describing a staged
post-training pipeline (long-CoT cold start, reasoning RL, “thinking mode fusion,” general RL, and strong-
to-weak distillation) (Team, 2025). In our evaluated set, Qwen3-4B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025c)
is a thinking-specialized checkpoint (no toggle) with a reported native 262K context window. Qwen3-30B-
A3B-Thinking-2507 (Qwen Team, 2025b) reduces active compute via sparse MoE (30.5B total parameters,
3.3B active; 128 experts with 8 active) while retaining the same reported 262K context length. Beyond the
evaluated roster, Qwen3-Next-80B-A3B-Thinking (Qwen Team, 2025d) extends this design with hybrid
attention and high-sparsity MoE; the model card attributes RL stability/efficiency in this regime to Group
Sequence Policy Optimization (GSPO) (Zheng et al., 2025). The model card also notes that “thinking” is
enforced by the default prompting template (automatic <think>) and that generations may contain longer
thinking traces than earlier Qwen3 variants.

D.3.3    NovaSky: high-quality distillation on a budget

NovaSky (UC Berkeley Sky Computing Lab) releases data recipes, verification code, training scripts, and
evaluation tooling alongside weights. The Sky-T1 line focuses on two objectives: inducing reliable long-CoT
behavior from instruction-tuned backbones with small verified corpora, and reducing overthinking-driven
token inflation.
Sky-T1-32B-Preview (Li et al., 2025a) serves as the anchor model for the series. Li et al. show that
Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct can acquire strong long-CoT behavior from a ∼17K set of demonstrations distilled
from QwQ-32B-Preview via rejection sampling, with large gains on math/code benchmarks and reported
competitiveness with o1-preview (NovaSky Team, 2025b). The paper reports that trace structure matters
more than trace content: perturbing surface tokens is less harmful than disrupting step order or logical
coherence (e.g., shuffling or deleting steps).


                                                           42
Sky-T1-32B-Flash (NovaSky Team, 2025a) targets deployment cost by explicitly penalizing overthinking.
It forms preference pairs from multiple sampled candidates per prompt (shortest-correct vs. longest-correct)
and adds hard-negative pairs (short incorrect vs. long correct) on difficult prompts; it then applies length-
sensitive preference optimization and optionally rewrites math solutions to retain only the first correct
solution plus one additional solution (“FCS+1”), while leaving code outputs unchanged. The preference
stage uses SimPO-style objectives (Meng et al., 2024), making reasoning-length control a direct training
target rather than a post-hoc decoding heuristic.
Sky-T1-7B (NovaSky Team, 2025c) explores smaller-budget post-training with an iterative SFT/RL sched-
ule. Starting from Qwen2.5-Math-7B, it performs SFT on a small verified set of QwQ-distilled traces,
applies RL with PRIME-style prompt filtering and rollouts on Eurus-2-RL-Data (Cui et al., 2025), re-distills
from the intermediate model for another SFT stage, and finishes with an RLOO-based RL stage (Ahmadian
et al., 2024).

D.3.4   Bespoke-Stratos: reasoning distillation as data curation

Bespoke-Stratos (Bespoke Labs, 2025b) frames reasoning post-training as a curation and verification prob-
lem: given a strong teacher that produces long-form traces, a comparatively small but verified corpus can
transfer substantial reasoning behavior via cross-entropy SFT. The pipeline distills DeepSeek-R1 traces
into Bespoke-Stratos-17k and fine-tunes Qwen2.5-Instruct backbones at multiple scales.
The core artifact is Bespoke-Stratos-17k (16.7K examples) (Bespoke Labs, 2025c), implemented in Be-
spoke Curator (Bespoke Labs, 2025a) and derived from a Sky-T1-style distillation recipe. Candidate
traces are sampled from DeepSeek-R1 and filtered by rejection sampling with task-specific verifiers (strict
answer checks for mathematics; unit-test execution for code), yielding supervision dominated by correct
trajectories rather than noise. This verifier-first design trades dataset scale for correctness, improving the
signal-to-noise ratio of SFT updates.
Two checkpoints instantiate the recipe. Bespoke-Stratos-32B (Bespoke Labs, 2025d) fine-tunes Qwen2.5-
32B-Instruct on Bespoke-Stratos-17k; Bespoke-Stratos-7B (Bespoke Labs, 2025e) applies the same
procedure to Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Reported results via Evalchemy (Guha et al., 2024) indicate size-
able gains over the base backbones on standard math and code benchmarks, making these models a sample-
efficient distillation baseline.

D.3.5   OpenThoughts/OpenThinker: scaling verified supervised reasoning data

OpenThoughts (Guha et al., 2025; OpenThoughts Team, 2025c) aims to make reasoning post-training repro-
ducible at the level of data recipes by releasing pipelines for synthetic trace generation, verification, ablations,
and evaluation alongside model weights. The central claim is that a large portion of open-weight reasoning
gains can be achieved with SFT-only training when the supervision data are verified and systematically
composed.
OpenThoughts-114K (OpenThoughts Team, 2025d) establishes the baseline. The pipeline generates long-
form traces over curated math/science/code/puzzle prompts using a strong teacher and applies aggressive
filtering to retain traces that are both correct and consistently formatted. The corresponding OpenThinker
models fine-tune Qwen2.5 instruction-tuned backbones with standard cross-entropy SFT and evaluate via
Evalchemy, enabling controlled comparisons of verified vs. unverified traces under matched training and
evaluation code. The paper identifies verification as a primary driver: it keeps supervision aligned so dataset
scaling remains productive rather than saturating on noisy traces.
The project then scales both volume and recipe quality. OpenThoughts2-1M (OpenThoughts Team,
2025e) expands to one million verified examples and trains OpenThinker2-32B (OpenThoughts Team,
2025a); the authors report matching DeepSeek-R1-Distill-32B on key reasoning benchmarks (e.g., AIME,
LiveCodeBench) while retaining a fully public pipeline. OpenThoughts3-1.2M (OpenThoughts Team,
2025f) centers on systematic recipe search (1,000+ controlled experiments) and switches the trace teacher to
QwQ-32B for improved small-model transfer. OpenThinker3-7B (OpenThoughts Team, 2025b), trained
SFT-only on the 1.2M mixture (reported as 850K math, 250K code, 100K science) (OpenThoughts Team,


                                                        43
2025f), is positioned as state-of-the-art open-data 7B reasoning on AIME’25, LiveCodeBench, and GPQA
Diamond without RL. In our evaluation, OpenThoughts is a controlled baseline because it isolates the effect
of verified supervised data from RL, reward modeling, and preference collection.
In the evaluated roster for this paper, the OpenThoughts family is represented by OpenThinker2-32B
and OpenThinker3-1.5B, giving a matched pair of large- and small-scale SFT-only baselines under the
OpenThoughts2 and OpenThoughts3 recipes, respectively.

D.3.6   OpenAI gpt-oss-20b: MoE reasoning with distillation+RL and deployment-oriented packaging

gpt-oss-20b (OpenAI, 2025b) is OpenAI’s medium-size open-weight reasoning model. It is a mixture-of-
experts transformer trained with large-scale distillation and reinforcement learning, targeting a favorable
accuracy–cost point for local or latency-sensitive deployment (OpenAI, 2025). The OpenAI API documen-
tation reports ∼ 21B total parameters with ∼ 3.6B active parameters per token, reflecting a design that
trades routing overhead for lower active compute than dense baselines (OpenAI, 2025a). Consistent with this
cost-aware framing, the inference interface exposes a reasoning-effort control with three levels (low, medium,
high) that trades off latency/token usage against accuracy without changing weights.
Operationally, OpenAI distributes weights on Hugging Face and documents native MXFP4 quantization
to reduce memory requirements (e.g., ∼ 16GB) (OpenAI, 2025b). The release also includes companion
“safeguard” models (OpenAI, 2025c) for policy-grounded safety reasoning use cases.

D.3.7   Microsoft Phi-4 reasoning models

Microsoft’s Phi-4 reasoning line specializes in a 14B foundation model via curated supervision and, for one
variant, a short outcome-based RL stage. Starting from Phi-4 (Abdin et al., 2024), Phi-4-reasoning (Abdin
et al., 2025) applies SFT on “teachable” prompts—diverse and challenging but within the student’s learnable
regime—paired with long-form reasoning demonstrations from a stronger teacher (reported as o3-mini). The
report highlights teachable-prompt selection as a primary lever for converting long-CoT supervision into
measurable gains and emphasizes inference-time compute allocation (longer reasoning budgets, repeated
sampling) as part of the intended operating regime.
Phi-4-reasoning-plus (Abdin et al., 2025) adds a short outcome-based RL stage that rewards final-answer
correctness on a verified math set, improving difficult reasoning while increasing average reasoning length
and token usage. Microsoft also releases compact variants to probe the small-model regime. Phi-4-mini-
reasoning (3.8B) (Xu et al., 2025) uses a staged recipe (mid-training on distilled long-CoT data, high-quality
long-CoT SFT, rollout-DPO preference optimization, and verifiable-reward RL for math). Phi-4-mini-
flash-reasoning (Microsoft, 2025) targets high-throughput long-context reasoning (64K) with a hybrid
SambaY design and Differential Attention; the accompanying work reports substantial decoding-throughput
gains (e.g., under vLLM) while maintaining strong reasoning performance, and the “flash” recipe achieves
its reported results without RL in that specific variant (Ren et al., 2025).

D.3.8   NVIDIA Nemotron reasoning models

NVIDIA’s open reasoning releases cover three distinct operating points relevant to this study.
OpenReasoning-Nemotron-1.5B is a compact reasoning model used as a small-scale open baseline in
math/code settings, while AceReason-Nemotron-1.1-7B focuses more explicitly on verifiable reason-
ing through the OpenMathReasoning and OpenCodeReasoning pipelines (Toshniwal et al., 2025; Moshkov
et al., 2025; Ahmad et al., 2025a;b; Liu et al., 2025d). Together they mark the “small dense reasoner” and
“verifiable-reasoning specialist” ends of NVIDIA’s lineup.
NVIDIA-Nemotron-Nano-9B-v2 (Basant et al., 2025) adds a different design point: a hybrid efficiency-
oriented model with controllable reasoning behavior. Relative to the smaller OpenReasoning and AceReason
checkpoints, Nano v2 is useful in evaluation because it tests whether hybrid architectures and reasoning tog-
gles can preserve reasoning quality while reducing active compute. In our model roster, the three Nemotron
variants therefore provide complementary evidence on small-model reasoning, verifiable post-training, and
efficiency-oriented reasoning control within one vendor family.


                                                     44
D.3.9    Open-R1 and OlympicCoder: open reproductions for math and competitive programming

Open-R1 (Hugging Face, 2025a) is an open, staged reproduction of DeepSeek-R1-style reasoning post-
training that emphasizes auditable artifacts (datasets, scripts, and intermediate checkpoints) to enable con-
trolled studies of data mixture, verification, and inference protocol choices. Methodologically, it serves as a
reference pipeline in which the relevant object of study is the full system (data + post-training + inference),
especially for reasoning benchmarks where test-time compute can materially change outcomes.
A central component is Mixture-of-Thoughts (Open-R1 Team, 2025b), a ∼350K dataset of verified traces
distilled from DeepSeek-R1 across math, code, and science. OpenR1-Distill-7B (Open-R1 Team, 2025e)
is trained by SFT on this mixture and provides a transparent baseline for analyzing inference-time scal-
ing (e.g., repeated sampling and aggregation) and subsequent verifiable-reward RL on a fixed checkpoint.
Open-R1 also provides a math-only corpus, OpenR1-Math-220k (Open-R1 Team, 2025f), derived from
NuminaMath 1.5 with multiple traces per problem and automatic verification via Math Verify; Llama-
3.3-70B-Instruct is used as a judge for the 12% of cases where rule-based checks are insufficient (Hugging
Face, 2025c).
OlympicCoder extends this methodology to competitive programming, where correctness is partially verifi-
able but the quality of supervision depends on test coverage. CodeForces-CoTs (Open-R1 Team, 2025a)
contains 10K Codeforces problems with up to five DeepSeek-R1 traces per problem (∼100K total); the
dataset documentation notes that traces are not exhaustively filtered for correctness, with about 84% of
Python solutions passing public tests (Hugging Face, 2025b). OlympicCoder-7B (Open-R1 Team, 2025d)
and OlympicCoder-32B (Open-R1 Team, 2025c) fine-tune Qwen2.5-Coder instruction models on de-
contaminated Codeforces-style CoT planning data and evaluate on standardized competitive-programming
benchmarks (including an IOI’2024 subset) and LiveCodeBench (Hugging Face, 2025d). Together, Open-
R1/OlympicCoder provides a transparent testbed for studying how verification strength, contamination
control, and inference-time computation interact in code reasoning.

D.3.10    GAIR “Less is More”: data-minimized reasoning and impact-aware RL

GAIR’s “Less is More” line argues that post-training efficiency is driven more by data selection and supervi-
sion structure than by raw volume. LIMO (Ye et al., 2025) demonstrates this with SFT of Qwen2.5-32B-
Instruct on 817 curated math problems paired with long, high-quality CoT solutions. The study formalizes
this as the Less-Is-More Reasoning Hypothesis and presents structured demonstrations and inference-time
compute allocation as complementary levers for extracting latent reasoning capability. GAIR subsequently
released LIMO-v2 (GAIR, 2025b), updating the training set to 800 examples (GAIR, 2025a) and releasing
a corresponding Qwen2.5-32B-Instruct fine-tune checkpoint. The updated paper describes the revised
set as selecting, for each problem, the highest-quality solution sampled from DeepSeek-R1, DeepSeek-
R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, and QwQ-32B, preserving the minimal-SFT philosophy while upgrading the trace
sources (Ye et al., 2025).
LIMR (Li et al., 2025b) transfers the same efficiency goal to RL by focusing on which prompts drive
learning. It introduces Learning Impact Measurement (LIM) to select high-impact training samples by
aligning reward trajectories with the model’s learning dynamics. LIMR reports that RL on a selected subset
of 1,389 prompts can match or exceed RL on the full 8,523-prompt pool, challenging the assumption that RL
gains require scaling prompt count. The paper also highlights a regime difference: extreme SFT minimization
is most effective at larger scales, whereas impact-aware RL remains effective at smaller scales when selection
maximizes learning signal (Li et al., 2025b).

D.3.11    FuseAI/FuseO1: parameter-space aggregation for behavior composition

Fusion and checkpoint merging provide a non-gradient route to composing reasoning behaviors by combining
trained “experts” directly in parameter space. Many reasoning checkpoints differ primarily in reasoning style
(long-CoT vs. concise “flash”) and domain emphasis; if interference is controlled, merging can approximate
multi-expert behavior in a single set of weights with negligible additional compute and no new supervision
data.


                                                      45
FuseAI (Wan et al., 2024a; 2025; Yang et al., 2025) studies heterogeneous model fusion via distribution-
level and chat-oriented pipelines and introduces SCE (Select–Calculate–Erase), a parameter-matrix merge
rule built around fusion vectors (weight differences from a pivot) and a structured per-matrix merge pro-
cedure. FuseO1 (FuseAI, 2025c;a;b) applies SCE-style merging to open reasoning checkpoints, combining
DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B, QwQ-32B-Preview, and Sky-T1-32B variants to retain long-CoT
competence while reducing verbosity when a Flash parent is included. The model cards report gains under
pass@1 and consistency-style evaluation and note that merging is data-free at merge time. Operationally, the
merges are typically implemented using mergekit (Arcee AI, 2024), which enables rapid iteration over merge
coefficients and parent selection. Branch–merge variants such as TinyR1 are discussed with the Qihoo360
models below.

D.3.12   Light-R1 and TinyR1: curriculum post-training and branch–merge distillation

Light-R1 and TinyR1 represent compute-conscious routes to long-CoT reasoning: curriculum-style post-
training to induce long-CoT behavior in backbones that lack it, and branch–merge distillation to compose
domain expertise.
Light-R1 (Wen et al., 2025) targets long-CoT training with an explicit staged pipeline. The report describes
a two-stage curriculum SFT followed by semi-on-policy DPO to stabilize long-form reasoning and shape re-
sponse style. In the “from scratch” setting, Light-R1-32B (Qihoo360, 2025b) starts from Qwen2.5-32B-
Instruct and is trained on math-focused data, with reported generalization beyond math to broader bench-
marks. The paper also emphasizes data leverage via a 3K “stage-2” hard long-CoT set: applying this set as
additional SFT substantially improves DeepSeek-distilled students, yielding Light-R1-7B-DS (Qihoo360,
2025d) and Light-R1-32B-DS (Qihoo360, 2025c) from DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen checkpoints. Fi-
nally, Light-R1 studies RL once long-CoT competence is present: using GRPO, it further improves Light-
R1-14B-DS (Qihoo360, 2025a) and reports the expected coupling between deeper reasoning and higher
reward (often with increased length).
TinyR1-32B-Preview (Sun et al., 2025) addresses a different lever: accuracy and cross-domain breadth at
fixed scale, via Branch–Merge Distillation. It distills DeepSeek-R1 into domain-specialized branches using
domain-specific SFT, then merges them to transfer complementary knowledge and improve generalization
beyond any single branch. TinyR1 reports consistent gains over DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B across
math/code/science and approaches teacher performance on AIME’24.

D.3.13   SimpleScaling s1.1 family: minimalist SFT and budget forcing for test-time scaling

SimpleScaling’s s1.1 family (Muennighoff et al., 2025) preserves the original s1 recipe of lightweight SFT
on a small, curated reasoning set together with budget forcing at inference time, while replacing the earlier
Gemini-generated traces with DeepSeek-R1 traces. Budget forcing can either truncate reasoning or extend it
by suppressing the end-of-thinking signal and appending “Wait,” making test-time compute directly control-
lable. The resulting recipe relies on stronger supervision and explicit inference control rather than additional
architectural or pipeline complexity.
Across the 1.5B, 3B, 7B, 14B, and 32B variants, s1.1 applies the same post-training recipe to Qwen2.5-
Instruct backbones without scale-specific methodological changes. The smaller checkpoints mainly illustrate
transfer across model sizes, whereas s1.1-32B serves as the primary reference model. The paper situates
budget forcing within a broader test-time scaling framework by combining sequential scaling with parallel
aggregation methods such as majority voting and REBASE, so the s1.1 recipe remains compatible with
sampling-and-aggregation protocols rather than a single decoding setup.

D.3.14   LG EXAONE-4.0: unified chat-reasoning modes and long-context post-training

EXAONE-4.0 treats chat-style assistance and explicit reasoning as two modes of a single model family rather
than as separate systems. LG implements this with a unified post-training recipe that jointly optimizes non-
reasoning and reasoning behavior, followed by preference-learning stages that balance correctness, brevity,


                                                      46
and language consistency. The emphasis is on aligning usability, deliberate reasoning, and agentic behavior

The 32B model also incorporates several targeted engineering choices. Instead of relying on fully global

reasoning quality without losing capabilities acquired during SFT. The tool-use setup follows the same design
principle, emphasizing multi-turn, long-horizon interaction with execution feedback rather than isolated tool
calls.

F     Software

Scorio exposes the aggregation protocol of Section 3.3 as three composable stages: confidence or reward
signals reduce trace-level metadata to candidate scores; fixed-bank rules select an answer from aligned answer
and score arrays; and online predicates determine whether sampling or generation should stop given only
the history observed so far. Keeping these stages separate permits the same candidate bank to be evaluated
under several decision rules, or the same rule to be paired with different evidence sources, without conflating
candidate generation with post-generation selection.
The interface accepts a one-dimensional candidate bank for a single problem or a two-dimensional array for
a batch. Score-free consensus, score-based selection, and across-rollout stopping share the same extracted-
answer representation. When a rationale must accompany the selected answer, the fixed-bank routines can
also return the index of a representative candidate. Listing 1 illustrates the core workflow: plurality and
Best-of-N can disagree on one bank, a score-aware vote interpolates between these extremes, and a causal
stopping predicate consumes only the answers revealed so far.
 1   from scorio import agg
 2
 3   answers = ["A", "A", "A", "B"]
 4   scores = [0.30, 0.30, 0.30, 0.85]
 5
 6   # Fixed-bank decisions over the same candidates
 7   plurality = agg.majority_vote(answers)                      # "A"
 8   best = agg.best_of_n(answers, scores)                       # "B"
 9   weighted = agg.softmax_weighted_vote(
10       answers, scores, temperature=0.1
11   )                                                           # "B"
12
13   # A stopping check reads only the prefix observed at decision time
14   observed = ["A"] * 8 + ["B"] * 2
15   stop, probability = agg.adaptive_consistency_stop(
16       observed, threshold=0.95, return_prob=True
17   )                                                        # (True, 0.9673...)

evidence.

Token-level fields. At every prompt and completion position, the full gzip archive stores token text,
ID, bytes, the chosen-token log probability and rank, and up to 20 candidate tokens with their IDs, bytes,
log probabilities, and ranks. The chosen token is one of those entries, not an additional twenty-first token.
Candidate arrays must be sorted numerically before being interpreted as ranked because vLLM may serialize
the sampled token first. Completion ranks are right-censored at 20, while prompt-token ranks may exceed
20.
The values are raw model log probabilities before presence penalties and sampling truncation. Renormalizing
GPT-OSS’s observed width-20 values recovers its effective categorical distribution in this capture because
temperature is one, full nucleus mass is retained, and no penalty is applied. The same is not generally true
for Qwen: its presence penalty can change membership before top-k and top-p filtering.
The compact Parquet view retains chosen-token and width-20 log-probability arrays but omits raw text and
candidate token identities; analyses that need token identity must read the full gzip archive. The validated
compact snapshot contains 1,832,922,842 completion positions and 36,658,456,840 candidate log-probability
values. Neither view contains a process reward or a validated answer-start boundary, so we do not run
process-reward or split-span methods.

Exact length-framed deduplication maps 104,160 archive locations to 97,786 unique question–reference–

Reference-free pointwise Qwen evaluator. The corpus-wide score adapts the rubric and expected-
score idea of LLM-as-a-Verifier to one candidate at a time (Kwok et al., 2026). It replaces the cited method’s
jointly prompted candidate pair and Gemini judge with one trajectory and Qwen, uses one critique and one
20-level score per criterion, and ranks candidates directly. It does not run pairwise orientations, repeated
comparisons, a tournament, or Bradley–Terry aggregation. For each of three criteria, Qwen first produces a
critique with thinking disabled, temperature zero, top-p = 1, top-k = 0, a 4,096-token cap, and <score> as
a stop string. A second request appends <score> as an assistant prefill and uses temperature 1, top-p = 1,
top-k = 0, a one-token cap, and a deterministic seed derived from the content hash and criterion. It requests
20 log-probability entries over the constrained bare and leading-space spellings of A–T; the sampled token is
ignored. Duplicate spellings are collapsed by their maximum probability, visible mass is renormalized, and
unobserved letters receive zero mass.

G.7    Field-balanced SuperGPQA extension

SuperGPQA contains 26,529 multiple-choice questions across 285 graduate disciplines (M-A-P Team et al.,
2025). Our 3,600-question materialization has 50 items in each of 72 EvalScope fields. It contains 1,447
easy, 1,607 middle, and 546 hard questions; 940 are marked as calculation questions. The deterministic
construction in Table 6 makes the sample rebuildable and ensures that large source fields do not dominate
the result.
The exact zero-shot user template is:
1   Answer the following multiple choice question. The last line of your response
2   should be of the following format: ’ANSWER: [LETTER]’ (without quotes) where
3   [LETTER] is one of {letters}. Think step by step before answering.
4
5   {question}
6
7   {choices}


The adapter extracts the final option letter and records binary accuracy. The four generation banks are
Qwen3.6 and correctly rendered gpt-oss low, medium, and high, each with seeds 0–79. Sampling, serving, and
trace capture follow the signal-rich mathematics protocol. The verifier fields use the same model snapshots
and score constructions: Compass traverses response-record locations, whereas pointwise Qwen deduplicates
within generator groups and reconciles cross-group duplicates. Its rubric is generalized to factual or technical
accuracy, logical coherence, and completeness. The complete design contains 3,600 × 4 × 80 = 1,152,000
responses.
The finalized gpt-oss high rule-outcome matrix provides one view of the heterogeneity that a single aggregate
hides. Its 288,000 responses have mean accuracy 45.03%. Exact Pass@80 is 81.94%, while pass80 is 9.47%.
Of the 3,600 questions, 650 are never answered correctly and 341 are answered correctly in all 80 responses.
